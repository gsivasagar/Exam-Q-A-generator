# Photosynthesis and Plants

## Light Reactions
Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose inside the chloroplast. Chlorophyll absorbs mostly red and blue light and reflects green light, which is why leaves look green. The light-dependent reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes and split water, releasing oxygen as a by-product. These reactions produce ATP and NADPH that are used in the next stage. Photosystem II captures light first and passes excited electrons along a transport chain to photosystem I.

## Calvin Cycle
The Calvin cycle takes place in the stroma of the chloroplast and does not need light directly. The enzyme rubisco fixes carbon dioxide onto a five-carbon sugar called RuBP. Three turns of the cycle are needed to produce one molecule of the three-carbon sugar G3P. The cycle uses ATP and NADPH from the light reactions to reduce the fixed carbon. Two G3P molecules combine to form one glucose molecule.

## Plant Transport
Water is absorbed by root hairs and travels upward through the xylem vessels. Transpiration is the evaporation of water from leaves, and it pulls water up the stem by cohesion and tension. The phloem carries dissolved sugars from the leaves to growing parts and storage organs in a process called translocation. Stomata are pores on the leaf surface that are opened and closed by guard cells. Guard cells swell with water to open the stomata and lose water to close them.

## Limiting Factors
The rate of photosynthesis is limited by light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature. At low light intensity the rate rises in direct proportion to the light supplied. High temperatures denature enzymes such as rubisco and cause the rate to fall. C4 plants such as maize concentrate carbon dioxide in special bundle sheath cells and waste less energy on photorespiration. Desert plants using CAM photosynthesis open their stomata only at night to save water.
